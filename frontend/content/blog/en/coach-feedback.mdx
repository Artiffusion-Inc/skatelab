## A review needs somewhere to lead

Watching a recording several times does not necessarily tell the skater what to do next. They can hear many valid comments and still return to the ice without a clear task. Start the conversation with one question that matters to the current practice, rather than trying to correct the entire movement at once.

This is not a universal teaching method. Technique, repetition count and training load are decisions for the coach who knows the skater. The topic here is conversation structure and preserving context, not prescribing an exercise from a clip.

## Describe what is visible first

Watch the whole attempt. Then return to the moment that raised the question and to the frames around it. Describe the observation in plain language: which part of the movement is visible, what changes between frames and where the available information ends.

Do not merge description and cause. “The support is hidden from this angle” is an observation about recording conditions. “The skater placed the skate incorrectly” is already a technique interpretation, which may need more evidence. Separating these levels makes the conversation more precise and leaves room for a check.

## Preserve the attempt’s context

Name the element, camera position and practice question. When comparing two recordings, check whether conditions are comparable. A different angle, scale or edit point can change the impression without any real change in movement.

One successful attempt does not establish a stable skill. One unsuccessful recording does not describe the skater as a whole. If an observation repeats, discuss it with the coach in the context of a series, not as a permanent label.

## Define the next step

A useful review note answers three questions: what did we notice, what did we agree to check, and what sign will let us revisit that decision? The coach chooses the sign. It needs to be understandable to the skater and observable under the agreed conditions.

Instead of many corrections, keep one main action for the next approach. Ask the skater to repeat the task in their own words. If the explanation has become a long sequence of technical terms, clarify it before returning to the ice. This is not a promise of faster progress; it is a way to check that both people understood each other.

## A note without invented results

A template can look like this:

- **Observation:** the moment reviewed and what was actually visible.
- **Limitation:** missing information or conditions that prevent comparison.
- **Task:** one action agreed with the coach.
- **Check:** which next recording and camera angle will be compared.

This is an empty structure, not a real athlete’s report or a ready-made prescription. It works in an ordinary notebook. The conversation supplies the value, not the presence of a digital interface.

## Return to the agreement

After the next attempt, begin with the previous question. Did the observable detail change? Were recording conditions similar enough? If not, note the limitation and do not present the comparison as proven progress. The coach can change the task or request another recording.

Saved notes should support practice, not become an unnecessary dossier. Limit access, leave out needless personal details and agree video storage in advance, especially where children are involved.
