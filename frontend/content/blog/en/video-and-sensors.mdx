## Not two ways to obtain one truth

Video and inertial sensors observe movement differently. A camera records light and object positions in an image. An inertial measurement unit usually includes an accelerometer and gyroscope: they record specific force and angular velocity in device coordinates. These are different raw signals, not two independent judgements of jump quality.

The useful first question is not which source is universally more accurate, but what you need to learn about this attempt. Visible footage is needed to discuss arm position in the frame. A gyroscope may help describe rapid changes in the device’s angular velocity. Neither source by itself explains why a skater made an error.

## What video contributes

A recording lets you return to the sequence, watch the approach and exit, and discuss body position that is actually visible. Neighbouring frames explain transitions better than one striking still. But the image remains a projection of a three-dimensional scene onto a plane.

Perspective, camera motion and body occlusion affect conclusions. An angle in the image is not necessarily an anatomical angle in space. If one skate hides the other, a model has no direct observation of that ice contact. An estimated invisible point is an inference with limitations, not a newly measured detail.

## What an inertial sensor contributes

A gyroscope describes rotation of the sensor, not automatically every body segment. An accelerometer does not output ready-made skating speed. Motion estimation needs to account for orientation, gravity, measurement bias and mounting. Integration can accumulate errors over time.

Even an estimated device orientation is not yet a reliable conclusion about skate edge use. The relationship between sensor axes and equipment, calibration and a checked method for the specific scenario all matter. Changing the mounting position changes that relationship. That is why the [equipment page](/equipment) does not promise that any tracker will produce an equivalent result.

## Align time before interpreting

To compare a signal with a frame, you must establish that they refer to the same moment. Camera and sensor clocks can drift, recordings may start at different times, and exports may change timestamps. A similar-looking peak on a chart is not proof of exact alignment.

Check the synchronisation method and original data before interpretation. If timing uncertainty is large relative to the event of interest, do not draw a conclusion about the exact order of actions. Explicitly keeping a limitation is better than presenting decimal places as reliability.

## Discuss the result with the coach

Separate three levels: what the instrument recorded, what processing calculated and what the coach concludes. Visible contact in a video, a selected phase boundary and a task for the next exit are not the same thing. Each level needs its own check.

When sources disagree, start with recording conditions: angle, occlusion, timeline, calibration and mounting. Do not automatically prefer the more technical-looking signal. Sometimes the useful outcome is “we need another recording”, not a rating of the skater.
